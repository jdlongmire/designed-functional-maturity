# Actualization, Agency, and Intelligibility: Foundations for a Designed Functional Maturity Programme

**Abstract**
This paper defends two arguments that a companion comparative paper assumes. The first is that actualization constitutes a genuine explanatory demand distinct from the demand physics answers: a formalism that constrains which states are admissible does not thereby explain why any state is actual. The second is that reality's intelligibility to human minds is better explained by a common rational source for knower and known than by cognitive faculties selected for fitness alone. Each argument is stated, then run against its strongest published opposition: Grünbaum's rejection of the question as ill-posed, van Inwagen's modal-collapse argument against the Principle of Sufficient Reason, Oppy's parity argument for a necessary natural initial state, Anscombe's criticism of the argument from reason, and Fitelson and Sober's technical objections to Plantinga. The paper concludes that the actualization argument survives in a weakened, non-contrastive form whose cost is stated explicitly, that the intelligibility argument survives better in abductive than in self-defeating form, and that a functional-maturity model of creation requires a fabrication criterion which, applied honestly, constrains the programme more tightly than its proponents have generally acknowledged.

---

## 1. Introduction

Three questions organize what follows. What is there? How can it be known? Why is it so? They correspond to ontology, epistemology, and ultimate explanation, and they are separable: a position on any one of them underdetermines the others.

A companion paper argues comparatively, surveying ten foundational features of reality and contending that methodological designism absorbs them with fewer residual explanatory relocations than methodological naturalism. That paper assumes two things it does not defend. It assumes that actualization is a real explanatory demand rather than a pseudo-question, and it assumes that agency is a category capable of answering it. Both assumptions are contested by serious philosophers, and neither is self-evident. This paper defends them, and marks where the defense is partial.

The order matters. The actualization argument, if it succeeds, delivers an agent sufficient to actualize. It does not deliver a rational, singular, or good agent, and it certainly does not deliver the God of Christian confession. Those are separate claims resting on separate evidence. Running the actualization argument and the intelligibility argument as one continuous movement, which is the natural way to present them and the way the source material for this paper presented them, obscures a seam that critics will find. They are therefore run here in sequence: §3 and §4 argue from contingency to agency; §5 argues from intelligibility to *rational* agency; §9 marks precisely where philosophical argument stops and a different kind of evidence would have to take over.

The paper is written to be falsifiable in the sense its research programme demands elsewhere. Section 8.4 names conditions under which its central model should be abandoned. A foundations paper for a programme governed by Popperian and Lakatosian discipline that supplied no such conditions would fail its own standard.

## 2. Initialization and Dynamics

Physical theory has two distinct inputs. It has dynamical laws, which specify how a state evolves into a successor state, and it has boundary conditions, which specify what there is for the laws to operate upon. No dynamical theory derives its own boundary conditions. This is not a controversial claim about physics; it is a structural feature of how physical theories are built.

Write the distinction schematically. Let

> S(t+1) = F_L(S(t))

represent lawful dynamical evolution, where L is the set of lawful regularities and F_L the evolution they specify. Physical science investigates F_L with extraordinary success: given a state and its regularities, what happens next?

Let

> S₀ = G(Φ, L, B)

represent initialization, where S₀ is the initialized state, B its boundary conditions, and Φ its commissioned functions. The origins question asks about G. Why is there an initialized physical reality at all, and why one with these regularities rather than others?

An analogy from systems engineering is useful and, being an analogy, is not an argument. When a functioning system is booted, it does not follow that every capability present at initialization was produced by runtime processes subsequently operating inside it. Initialization and subsequent state transition are different categories, and inferring the content of the former exclusively from extrapolation of the latter is a category confusion, not an inference. The analogy illustrates the distinction; it does not establish that anything in particular was initialized rather than evolved. That question is empirical, and the companion paper treats it.

The distinction itself is uncontroversial. Albert (2000) makes it in exactly this form when he argues that statistical mechanics requires a Past Hypothesis as a further postulate beyond dynamics and probabilistic indifference, because the dynamics plus the statistics do not deliver the low-entropy initial macrostate. Where controversy begins is over whether the second input, having been isolated, *needs explaining*. That is the subject of §3.

## 3. First Argument: Constraint Is Not Actualization

### 3.1 The claim

A mathematical formalism can specify which states are admissible. It does not follow that the formalism explains why any admissible state is actual. Admissibility is a modal property; actuality is not. Between the space of admissible states and the one that obtains there is a gap, and nothing in the specification of the space closes it.

This yields a hierarchy: representable → logically admissible → physically admissible → actualized → ordered states → physical history. Each level constrains the next. No level actualizes the next.

### 3.2 The claim is not distinctively theistic

It matters that this observation does not originate with, and is not confined to, defenders of theism. Hawking closes *A Brief History of Time* by asking what "breathes fire into the equations and makes a universe for them to describe," and adds that "the usual approach of science of constructing a mathematical model cannot answer the questions of why there should be a universe for the model to describe" (Hawking, 1988). He poses the gap and leaves it open.

The sharpest statement comes from a naturalist. Reviewing Krauss's *A Universe from Nothing*, Albert (2012) argues that relativistic quantum field-theoretic vacuum states "are particular arrangements of elementary physical stuff," so that the true equivalent of there being no physical stuff "isn't this or that particular arrangement of the fields," but "the simple absence of the fields." His conclusion is unambiguous: if what was taken for nothing "turns out, on closer examination, to have the makings of protons and neutrons and tables and chairs and planets and solar systems and galaxies and universes in it, then it wasn't nothing, and it couldn't have been nothing, in the first place." Albert is not defending theism. He is objecting, as a philosopher of physics, that a theory describing how structure arises within an existing physical regime has not explained the existence of that regime.

Parfit (1998) makes the structural version of the same point: any causal explanation "necessarily leaves its own starting conditions unexplained," and the failure of what he calls the Null Possibility to obtain would require a Selector. Smolin (2013), an avowed naturalist, denies formalism any generative power at all: mathematics "comes after nature," and "has no generative power."

The gap, then, is not a theistic artifact. What is distinctively theistic is a particular proposal about what fills it.

### 3.3 The opposition

Three serious objections deny the demand rather than answering it. Each is stronger than the popular version of itself.

**Grünbaum: the question is ill-posed.** Grünbaum (2004, 2009) holds Leibniz's demand to be "an ill-conceived non-starter" posing a pseudo-issue. The argument is not that the question is hard but that it smuggles in an unwarranted premise: that nothingness is the natural, default, or spontaneous state, so that existence is the anomaly requiring a cause. Strip that premise and the question loses its bite. Our intuitions about what is spontaneous derive entirely from experience within an existing world, and give us no purchase on the comparative naturalness of a null world.

**Van Inwagen: the Principle of Sufficient Reason entails modal collapse.** Take the conjunction of all contingent truths, the Big Contingent Conjunction. The PSR demands it have an explanation. If the explanans is necessary, the BCC follows from a necessary truth and is itself necessary, so nothing is contingent. If the explanans is contingent, it is one of the BCC's own conjuncts and explains itself, which is circular. Either way the PSR fails (van Inwagen, 1983, pp. 202–204). This is the most technically serious argument against the principle the actualization demand relies on.

**Van Inwagen again: the probabilistic dissolution.** If there is more than one possible world there are infinitely many; at most one contains no concrete beings; and if the worlds are equiprobable, the empty world has probability zero (van Inwagen, 1996/2001). Nothingness is not impossible, merely maximally improbable, and no agent is required to explain why it does not obtain. Van Inwagen himself distrusts the argument as too simple, and Rodriguez-Pereyra attacks its second and fourth premises, but it remains a live naturalist dissolution.

**Oppy: parity with an Ockham premium.** The strongest objection is not "who caused God." It is Oppy's (2006, 2010) taxonomy-plus-parity argument. He enumerates the possible causal shapes of reality (infinite regress, causal circle, contingent initial state, necessary initial state) and argues that on each shape naturalism is at least the equal of theism, because whatever explanatory terminus the theist assigns to God (uncaused, self-existent, necessary) the naturalist may assign to an initial natural state, while positing strictly fewer entities. Being uncaused, Oppy argues, is a coherent privilege of an *initial* state as such, which exposes the theist's exemption for God as unmotivated rather than principled. This is a comparative-theoretical argument turning on parsimony and explanatory scope. Refuting the naive tu quoque leaves it entirely untouched.

### 3.4 What survives

Grünbaum's objection is the most easily met, though not defeated. The actualization demand as stated here does not require that nothingness be the default. It requires only that the actual state be one among admissible alternatives and that no feature of the admissible space singles it out. That is a claim about *selection among candidates*, not about the metaphysical privilege of a null world, and it survives Grünbaum's diagnosis because it does not employ the premise he identifies.

Van Inwagen's modal-collapse argument is more damaging and cannot be dismissed. The available responses are to weaken the principle rather than defend it whole. Gale and Pruss (1999) run their cosmological argument on a Weak PSR, requiring only that every fact *possibly* has an explanation, precisely to evade this objection; Koons (1997) treats the PSR as a defeasible presumption rather than a universal principle. This paper adopts the weaker commitment. What is claimed is not that every contingent fact has a sufficient reason but that the actualization of a physical order is the *kind* of fact for which an explanation may reasonably be sought, and that declining to seek one is a substantive position rather than a neutral default. Oppy has published targeted replies to both Gale-Pruss and Koons, and a full treatment would engage them; that is deferred work, and marking it as deferred rather than as handled is part of the point.

Van Inwagen's probabilistic dissolution addresses a different question than the one asked here. It explains why *something* rather than nothing, given an equiprobable measure over worlds. It does not explain why *this* world rather than another, and it purchases its result from an assumption about measure that is exactly what is at issue when the question is selection among admissible states.

Oppy's parity argument is the one that decides the matter, and the honest verdict is that it is not defeated but narrowed. The theist's reply cannot be that the naturalist has failed to terminate the regress, because the naturalist has terminated it, and with fewer posits. The reply must be that the two termini have different *explanatory shapes*. A brute initial state does not answer the selection question; it instantiates one option and declines to say why that one. An agent is the only category we possess in which selection among alternatives is what the thing characteristically does. Whether that constitutes a genuine advantage or merely relocates the mystery under a more flattering description is the subject of §4, and the answer there is more qualified than defenders of the argument usually allow.

## 4. The Agency Proposal and Its Central Difficulty

### 4.1 Agent causation is a standing category

The proposal is that actualization requires a primitive not present in the physical inventory, and that the primitive is agency:

> A(Ψ | L) → S₀

An agent A, given the admissible state space Ψ under constraints L, actualizes S₀.

This does not invent a category. Reid (1788) holds that agent causation is the only genuine or "efficient" causation, a cause being a being with power to produce an effect who exerts that power for a purpose; his anti-Humean formulation is that "the rules of navigation never navigated a ship." Chisholm (1964) gives the canonical modern statement, distinguishing immanent causation, in which a substance causes, from transeunt causation, in which an event causes, and concluding that "each of us, when we act, is a prime mover unmoved." Lowe (2008) defends a hybrid of volitionism and classical agent-causalism while attacking the causal-closure argument for physicalism. Most directly, O'Connor (2000, 2008) develops an agent-causal metaphysics and then applies it to the cosmological question, defending a Leibnizian contingency argument for a necessary being as ultimate explanation. The schema above has a direct ancestor in that work.

### 4.2 The objection that lands

Two objections are standard. The first, from Broad (1934), presses timing: "How could an event possibly be determined to happen at a certain date if its total cause contained no factor to which the notion of date has any application?" This bites the present schema, which posits a non-temporal selector of a determinate outcome, though §7 argues the bite is less severe than it appears once temporal priority is distinguished from temporal precedence.

The second is decisive enough to require a direct answer, and it is not the one most critics of theism raise. Consider two scenarios identical in every respect up to the moment of actualization, in one of which the agent actualizes S₀ and in the other S₀'. Nothing prior to that moment accounts for the difference. The regress the argument was built to stop has reattached one level up: *why that one?* Van Inwagen and Mele press that agent causation "would not solve the problem of transforming an undetermined event into one which is in our control"; Pereboom (2001) grants coherence but adds an empirical objection; Clarke (2003) argues substance causation collapses into event causation once substances act through their properties.

The reviewer of O'Connor (2008) in *Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews* states the dilemma in precisely the form that matters here. Either there is no probability distribution over the agent's possible intentions, in which case the naturalist may object that the theist loses entitlement to the claim that the agent *produces* anything; or there is such a distribution, in which case the advantage over a naturalist brute fact evaporates. The dilemma is structural. It is the paper's central burden, and it will not be discharged by restating the schema more formally.

### 4.3 The answer, and what it costs

The answer available is that non-contrastive explanation is still explanation, and that this is not special pleading because it is how agent-explanation works generally.

When a person selects one of two indistinguishable options, there is typically no further fact about the person, prior to the selection, that determined which. The selection is nonetheless explained, and explained *by the person*, in a way that a coin's landing is not explained by the coin. What agent-explanation supplies is not a contrastive antecedent but a locus of origination: an answer to "what did this?" rather than to "what made this rather than that inevitable?" The claim is that "what did this?" is a legitimate explanatory question with a real answer, and that a brute fact does not answer it because a brute fact does not *do* anything.

Three things must be said honestly about this move.

First, it weakens the argument. The conclusion is no longer that agency explains actualization in the full contrastive sense; it is that agency supplies a kind of explanation, origination, that a brute initial state cannot supply even in principle. That is a real asymmetry, but a narrower one than the argument is usually presented as delivering.

Second, the move is contested, and the agent-causal literature has generally *asserted* rather than *argued* that contrastive explanation is not owed. Treating that assertion as established would be exactly the failure this paper attributes to others.

Third, Oppy's reply is available and must be conceded to have force: if the theist is permitted a non-contrastive terminus, why is the naturalist not permitted one? The answer is that the naturalist's terminus is not merely non-contrastive but non-explanatory in the relevant respect, since it does not originate anything, it simply obtains. Whether that difference is substantive or verbal is, at present, an open question in the literature rather than one this paper can close.

The honest summary is that the actualization argument establishes less than its proponents claim and more than its critics allow. It does not demonstrate an agent. It shows that the choice between an agentive and a brute terminus is a choice between two kinds of explanatory stopping-point with different properties, and that the naturalist's parity claim requires the further premise that origination is not an explanatory desideratum. That premise is defensible. It is not obvious, and it is not free.

## 5. Second Argument: Intelligibility

### 5.1 Two distinct data

The first argument, at best, delivers an agent. The second is what would deliver a *rational* agent, and it is independent: it would retain force even if the actualization argument failed entirely.

Two distinct explananda must be kept apart, because conflating them is a common error that invites a decisive objection.

The first is object-side. The world is describable by mathematics, including mathematics developed on aesthetic grounds with no application in view. This is Wigner's (1960) puzzle, and it is a fact about the world.

The second is subject-side. Human cognitive faculties, if they arose through processes selected for reproductive fitness, were not selected for truth-tracking in domains remote from fitness. Yet they appear to track truth in cosmology, number theory, and quantum mechanics, none of which bear on ancestral survival.

These can come apart in both directions. A world could be mathematically transparent yet inhabited by unreliable minds, or opaque to reliable ones. Keeping them separate is not a fastidiousness; it protects the argument from the objection that it double-counts one datum.

### 5.2 Run it as abduction, not self-defeat

Plantinga's Evolutionary Argument Against Naturalism (1993, ch. 12; 2011, ch. 10) is the most developed subject-side treatment. Its main argument is not that naturalism-plus-evolution is false but that it is *self-defeating*: a low or inscrutable probability for the reliability of one's cognitive faculties, conditional on naturalism and evolution, defeats belief in that reliability, which in turn defeats everything else believed, including naturalism and evolution.

The self-defeat form has taken heavy fire, and much of it lands. Fitelson and Sober (1998) argue that setting the prior probability of reliability near unity is self-undermining, since on Bayesian grounds an observation of probability near one cannot confirm anything; that reliability is underspecified and, decomposed, receives low probability on *any* theory; that a low conditional probability does not establish low probability relative to total evidence; and that the argument, run consistently, would tell against traditional theism as well. Their parity point is narrower than it is often reported to be: not that evolution equally undermines the theist, but that neither side can construct a non-question-begging refutation of global skepticism.

The same lesson appears in the argument's earlier history. Lewis's (1947) version was criticized by Anscombe (1948) on three counts: that natural causes are non-rational rather than irrational, so a belief with non-rational causes needs no doubting absent a specific defeater; that "valid" equivocated; and that causes and grounds were conflated. Lewis conceded the second explicitly, calling "valid" a bad word for what he meant and preferring "veridical," and rewrote the chapter for the 1960 edition, retitling it and replacing most of its content. Anscombe herself described the exchange as "a sober discussion of certain quite definite criticisms" and judged the revision a real improvement while retaining objections. It was not a rout, and reporting it as one would be inaccurate in either direction.

The defensible structure is the one Reppert (2003) identifies and Lewis's own revision performs: run the argument as an inference to the best explanation rather than as a skeptical threat. The claim is not that the naturalist is rationally obligated to doubt everything. It is that the reliability of human cognition in fitness-irrelevant domains is a datum, that it is *expected* if knower and known derive from a common rational source, and that it is *surprising but not impossible* otherwise. Abduction is the right form because the datum is a fit between two things, and fits are what abduction handles.

### 5.3 The counter, and an unexpected complication

The mainstream reply is evolutionary epistemology: selection favors accurate world-modeling, because organisms that model the world badly die. Quine (1969, p. 126) states it memorably: "Creatures inveterately wrong in their inductions have a pathetic but praiseworthy tendency to die before reproducing their kind."

This is a genuine answer for fitness-relevant domains. Its weakness is that it extends poorly to the domains at issue. Nothing in ancestral selection pressure bears on whether human minds can model events at redshift eleven or prove theorems about transfinite cardinals, and the reply must therefore claim these capacities as spandrels of faculties selected for other purposes. That is possible. It is not obviously more parsimonious than the alternative, and it is asserted rather than demonstrated.

A complication cuts unexpectedly. Stich (1990), a naturalist, argues that a reliable cognitive system may be costlier and slower than an unreliable one, so fitness need not track truth. Prakash et al. (2021) formalize a stronger version: their Fitness Beats Truth theorem shows that in evolutionary game-theoretic models a fitness-only strategy generically drives a truth-estimating strategy to extinction, with the dominance increasing as perceptual space grows. This supports the argument's premise from within naturalism, which is rhetorically valuable. It must be handled carefully, since the result concerns *perceptual* veridicality under fitness payoffs rather than the reliability of theoretical reason, and Hoffman draws a conscious-realist rather than theistic conclusion. It supports the premise by analogy, not entailment, and should be cited that way.

The intelligibility argument, run abductively, survives its opposition in better condition than the actualization argument survives Oppy. It does not compel. It establishes that a fit exists between rational minds and an intelligible world, that theism predicts this fit antecedently, and that naturalism accommodates it. Prediction is worth more than accommodation, which is the whole substance of the claim and the limit of it.

## 6. Epistemic Categories

The question of how any of this could be known requires distinctions that are frequently collapsed.

**Observation.** Redshifts, the cosmic microwave background, isotope ratios, stratigraphic sequences, fossils, genomes, stellar spectra. These are data.

**Interpretation.** What the data indicate, given a theoretical framework. Theory-ladenness is not a defect and does not make interpretation arbitrary; it is a condition of data being informative at all.

**Historical reconstruction.** Claims about unobserved past states inferred from present observations under models incorporating assumptions about the constancy of processes.

These are genuinely distinct, and the distinction cuts in a direction its users often neglect. Reconstructions are not thereby unreliable. Many are extraordinarily well constrained, and the constraint is often multiply independent: radiocarbon calibration converges with dendrochronology, varve counting, speleothem records, and coral banding (Reimer et al., 2020). A framework invoking the observation/reconstruction distinction to weaken conclusions it dislikes, while accepting equally reconstruction-dependent conclusions it likes, is not applying a principle. It is selecting a rhetorical tool. The distinction is worth drawing only if applied symmetrically.

**Revelation.** If a creator exists and has communicated, testimony from that source would be evidence about origins. The epistemic point is narrow and, so stated, defensible: there is no neutral ground from which such testimony can be excluded *before* assessing whether it is genuine, since the exclusion presupposes an answer to the question at issue. What does not follow is that any particular claimed revelation is genuine, or that its interpretation is settled once its authority is granted. Both require separate argument, and neither is attempted here. A programme that treats "revelation is admissible in principle" as though it established "this text, read this way, is authoritative" has skipped the step that does the work.

## 7. Temporal Priority Without Temporal Precedence

An intuition obstructs the initialization/dynamics distinction: that whatever explains the universe's existence must be an earlier event, occurring while some clock already ticks. If physical time is a feature of physical reality, that picture may embed a category error. Creation could be logically prior to physical history without being an earlier event within it.

This is a conceptual point, and it stands or falls conceptually. It is worth noting, though, that the intuition is not mandatory even within physics, and here the claim must be stated with more care than it usually receives.

In canonical quantum gravity, the Hamiltonian constraint is standardly written ĤΨ = 0, an equation on the wave functional of three-geometry containing no external time parameter (DeWitt, 1967). This generates the "problem of time," a recognized open problem with a substantial literature (Isham, 1993; Kuchař, 1992; Anderson, 2017). Time must be recovered relationally (Page and Wootters, 1983; Rovelli, 2002), semiclassically, or otherwise, and the approaches divide into at least four families that do not agree.

Five hedges are required, and omitting any of them would be overreach.

There is no settled theory of quantum gravity. The Wheeler-DeWitt equation is not a rigorous object: in the general case it multiplies functional derivatives at a point and requires regularization, factor ordering is ambiguous, and no accepted inner-product measure exists. It is well-defined only in symmetry-reduced minisuperspace models, and DeWitt himself is reported to have said in 1997 that it should be "confined to the dustbin of history." Emergent time is one live option among several, not an established result; Bamonti, Cinti and Sanchioni (2025) argue the semiclassical time parameter is underdetermined, with multiple equally good candidates, and emergent only pointwise rather than globally. The problem is specific to *canonical* quantization, so the honest formulation is "at least one serious research programme," not "physics." And a reviewer may reasonably respond that the timelessness in question is inherited from classical diffeomorphism invariance, in which case nothing has been discovered that general covariance did not already contain.

With those hedges the claim reduces to something modest and defensible in existential form: our intuitive picture of a cosmic clock ticking while the universe comes into being is *not required* even by serious physical theorizing. That is all that is claimed. Nothing here proves Genesis, supports theism, or establishes that time is emergent. The section's function is defensive, dissolving an objection rather than supporting a conclusion, and a reader who takes it for more has been misled by presentation rather than by argument.

## 8. Functional Maturity and the Fabrication Criterion

### 8.1 The objection

If creation deploys systems already functional, the question arises whether it thereby deploys evidence of a history that did not occur. Gosse (1857) faced this directly, distinguishing *diachronic* time, actual and post-creation, from *prochronic* time, ideal or virtual, existing only in the divine idea yet recorded in the created world's marks. Tree rings, worn teeth, and Adam's navel were, for Gosse, prochronic necessities rather than optional additions; his argument was that no organism *could* be created without such marks.

The reception was almost uniformly hostile from scientists and clergy alike, and the idea effectively vanished within a year (Krause, 1980). Kingsley's objection was moral rather than logical: absolute creation of this kind makes God *Deus quidam deceptor*, and "in the one single case of your newly created scars on the pandanus trunk, your newly created Adam's navel, you make God tell a lie. It is not my reason, but my conscience which revolts here."

Russell (1921) supplies the reductio. "There is no logical impossibility in the hypothesis that the world sprang into being five minutes ago, exactly as it then was, with a population that 'remembered' a wholly unreal past." He notes it is logically tenable and uninteresting. That is the danger: a framework permitting arbitrary apparent history permits this, and a framework permitting this has abolished historical evidence altogether, including the historical evidence on which any claimed revelation depends.

It is worth recording that the standard dismissals of Gosse are weaker than usually assumed. Bennett (2020) argues that both the Occam's-razor objection and the unfalsifiability objection fail, and that Omphalos was rejected for theological unattractiveness rather than logical defect. The objection that survives is Kingsley's, and it is about honesty rather than parsimony.

### 8.2 The criterion

The distinction the framework needs is between **functional maturity** and **fabricated history**.

A system deployed to perform a commissioned function must possess whatever that function requires at the moment of deployment. A mature tree requires structural wood. An adult requires adult physiology. Wine requires the chemistry of wine. These are not deceptions; they are what it is for the thing to be the thing.

Fabricated history is different in kind. It is a specific, internally consistent record of particular events that did not occur, present in the artifact beyond what its commissioned function requires. A mature adult carries no false autobiographical memories. Wine carries no vintage record naming a harvest that never happened.

The criterion follows: **required-for-commissioned-function is admissible; gratuitous event-record is not.** A creator may deploy a functional system. A creator who embeds a detailed false event log has done something the first act does not entail, and Kingsley's objection applies to the second and not the first.

Two things must be said about the status of this criterion. It is not novel: it has been asserted in popular creationist writing at least since Harold (2017), who states it in nearly these terms. And it has never been developed philosophically; no treatment in academic philosophy of religion was located. Duff (2021) argues that the distinction collapses precisely because its proponents "have been at a loss to provide a functional definition of how that created maturity is fundamentally different than creation with the appearance of history of specific events." That critique is fair against an unelaborated assertion. Whether it is fair against a criterion stated as above depends entirely on whether the criterion can be applied to hard cases and yield determinate verdicts, including unwelcome ones.

### 8.3 Applying it against interest

The hardest case is light in transit, and the criterion gives a split verdict rather than a convenient one.

Light already en route at deployment is plausibly required-for-function on a reading where the luminaries' commissioned function is to give light upon the earth. A luminary that will not illuminate for millennia is not performing its commissioned function, and light in transit is what performing it consists in. So far the criterion licenses what the framework wants.

But light does not merely illuminate. It carries structured information about specific events. A supernova light curve encodes a particular star's particular death, with a characteristic rise, decay, and spectral evolution. Eclipsing-binary light curves encode specific orbital histories. These are not required for illumination. Illumination requires photons in transit; it does not require that those photons encode a detailed event record. On the criterion as stated, such records fall on the gratuitous side of the line, and the framework must either accept that the events they record actually occurred, with the temporal consequences that entails, or accept Kingsley's charge for those cases.

This is not an outsider's objection. Faulkner (2026), writing as Answers in Genesis' own astronomer, concedes the point: on a mature-creation account of starlight, eclipsing binaries mean "we are seeing evidence of processes that never happened," and "that seems deceptive."

The alternatives within young-earth astronomy do not currently relieve the difficulty. Humphreys' white-hole cosmology has been abandoned by Humphreys himself and replaced twice. Lisle's anisotropic synchrony convention is untestable by its author's own account, is described as "somewhat ad hoc" by Creation Ministries International, and has drawn an internal-contradiction critique in *Answers Research Journal*. Light-speed decay is rejected within young-earth circles. Mainstream engagement with any of these is effectively nil.

The consistent conclusion is that the fabrication criterion, honestly applied, constrains the programme more tightly than its proponents have generally acknowledged. It licenses functional maturity. It does not license arbitrary apparent history, and it does not obviously license the young-earth astronomical case. A criterion that returned only convenient verdicts would not be a criterion, and the programme's stated commitment to symmetric rigor requires reporting this rather than routing around it.

### 8.4 Discriminators

The model's falsification conditions follow from the criterion.

The framework should be abandoned, or substantially revised, if any of the following obtains. If no principled line can be drawn between required-for-function and gratuitous event-record that yields determinate verdicts across cases, the criterion is vacuous and the deceiver objection stands unanswered. If the criterion, consistently applied, rules against the model's own commitments across a broad range of domains rather than in isolated cases, the model is being maintained by exemption rather than by evidence. If functional maturity is invoked *ad hoc*, domain by domain, whenever conventional reconstruction conflicts with the model, rather than being derived antecedently from stated commissioned functions, it has become an unfalsifiable accommodation and should be treated as one.

Section 8.3 is a partial instance of the second condition, and is recorded as such rather than absorbed.

## 9. What the Argument Delivers

Stated precisely, and marking where each step stops:

**To be.** A real, ordered, informationally structured physical reality exists and is contingent, in the minimal sense that its existence and character are not self-explanatory.

**Why.** Actualization is a legitimate explanatory demand, though a defeasible rather than universal principle of sufficient reason is what survives van Inwagen's modal-collapse objection. Agency is a real and standing category capable of supplying origination where a brute terminus cannot. It supplies non-contrastive explanation only, and Oppy's parity objection is narrowed rather than defeated. What is established is that the choice between agentive and brute termini is a real choice with real explanatory consequences, not that the agentive terminus is demonstrated.

**Rationality.** The intelligibility argument, run abductively, gives reason to prefer a *rational* source: the fit between rational minds and an intelligible world is predicted by theism and accommodated by naturalism, and prediction is worth more than accommodation. This does not compel.

**To know.** Reason, observation, historical inference, and, if genuine, revelation. The categories are distinct and the distinction must be applied symmetrically or not at all.

**How.** Initialization establishes S₀; lawful dynamics describe the subsequent history. Which features of the present order were initialized rather than developed is an empirical question that this paper does not answer and the companion comparative paper takes up.

Here is the seam. Everything above delivers, at most, a rational agent sufficient to actualize a contingent order. It does not deliver a singular agent, a good agent, an agent who acts in history, or the God of Christian confession. The identification of the agent is a separate claim resting on separate evidence: the historical credentials of a claimed revelation, its internal coherence, and its explanatory fruitfulness. That case can be made, and this paper's author holds that it can be made well, but it is not made here and nothing above should be read as making it. The Logos of John 1:3 is a theological identification of the agent this argument leaves unidentified, and treating the philosophical primitive and the theological identification as a single move is the equivocation critics will look for first.

---

## 10. Conclusion

Two arguments have been defended at unequal strength.

The actualization argument establishes that a formalism constraining admissible states does not explain why any is actual, that this observation is not a theistic artifact but is pressed by Hawking, Albert, Parfit, and Smolin, and that agency is a standing philosophical category supplying a kind of explanation, origination, unavailable to a brute terminus. It does not establish an agent. Its central difficulty, that the contrastive regress reattaches at the agent, is answered by arguing that non-contrastive agent-explanation is genuine explanation, at the cost of a weaker conclusion than the argument is usually said to yield.

The intelligibility argument fares better, provided it is run as abduction rather than as a self-defeat argument, since the technical objections of Fitelson and Sober bite the latter and largely miss the former.

The functional-maturity model requires a criterion distinguishing required-for-function from gratuitous event-record. That criterion is stateable, has not previously been developed, and when applied honestly constrains the programme more than its proponents have granted, including against the young-earth astronomical case.

The central proposition is narrower than the framework's rhetoric usually suggests and, so narrowed, is defensible: possibility does not explain actuality; law does not explain why there is something for law to govern; description does not supply origination. Whether agency, and ultimately Logos, provides a more coherent account of the evidence than the alternatives is a comparative question. It is not settled by this paper, which aims only to establish that the question is well-formed and that the leading answer has not been eliminated.
